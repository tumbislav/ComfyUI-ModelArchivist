# The librarians

## Invitation

It started simply, as most things do: I wanted to have a cover image that would conjure up the idea of an archivist or a librarian. Ok, but which librarian? Which library, which archive? 

I started to generate pictures, mostly at random. That is how rabbit holes are dug. Once I had a few, I wanted to know the people in them. Who are they, what are their lives, their problems? Are they real? A couple of weeks spent burrowing through my (bookshelves)  and dusty corners of the Internet made them real, some of them far more real than I expected.

So yes, you are invited to play: scour the history of humanity and the weird corners of your imagination and bring back your own librarians. It's a game, and here are the rules:

* Provide an image of the librarian and the library in 1600x640px resolution. The library or archive must be on the right and the librarian on the left, preferably facing the library.

* Make sure your maiden aunt wouldn't be *too* shocked by the image.
* If the librarian is a living person, the usual rules of decency apply: get their consent and don't embarrass anyone.

* If the librarian is a historical person, *check the facts and bring the references.*

* If the librarian is fictional, but set in a real time and place, *check the facts and bring the references.*

* If you're making things up, go for it.

* The vignette should be up to a hundred words, give or take. The idea is to invoke a real moment of life, even if it's fictional.
* Really good contributions will be included in the next release of the app. The selection process will be opaque, arbitrary and entire left to the whim of the maintainer, although bribes, wheedling and inappropriate pictures will be considered.

## The facts and the references

### Kunnaniya

Kunnaniya and her amazing story are real. We know her from the letters she wrote and received, and from others who mentioned her in her letters. and contracts. She lived in 19th century BCE in central Anatolia. When her husband Ashur-muttabbil died, she travelled travelled more than 1100 km from her home in [Kanesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kültepe) to [Akkad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akkad_(city)), to get Ashur-muttabil's family to cough up her part of the inheritance. 

She was rebuked. We lose trail of her about the time when she wrote that desperate letter to her sister. The remainder of the story is fiction, but plausible. 

Check out [The Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/326712) for one of the letters, and also check out Cecile Michel's excellent [Women of Assur and Kanesh](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctv17ppd1s).
### Za-Walda and Mäkwännät

In the third century CE, the [kingdom of Aksum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Aksum) was considered the fourth great power in the world, after China, Persia and the Roman empire. Yes, I didn't know that either. It controlled the trade between the Mediterranean world and India. If you're feeling nerdy, [here's a good source](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/foundations-of-an-african-civilisation/some-written-sources-relating-to-aksumite-civilisation/5FD9B5865A0BE1CEBB2A8537888CAA32), and, if you insist on original sources, check out [Periplus](https://depts.washington.edu/silkroad/texts/periplus/periplus.html).

Za-Walda and Mäkwännät are fictional, but there could well have been merchants like them that made that trade happen.

### Berenike

The great [library of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library_of_Alexandria) needs no introduction. Berenike is a figment of the author's imagination, very loosely inspired by [Hypatia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypatia), who lived many century later. Berenike herself lived in 2<sup>nd</sup> century BCE, at a time when [Conics](https://archive.org/details/treatiseonconics00apolrich/page/n5/mode/2up) of [Apollonius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollonius_of_Perga) were the bleeding edge of mathematics.

### Sovann

In the late 13th century, [Angkor Wat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angkor_Wat) was changing from a Hindu temple complex to a Buddhist one. At more or less the same time, there was a flurry of intellectual activity in the [Theravada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theravada) community in Sri Lanka. Their manuscripts would have been of great interest to our fictional Sovann, but they were written in Pāli, so Moggallāna’s [Abhidhānappadīpikā](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abhidh%C4%81nappad%C4%ABpik%C4%81) would have been a great resource.

### Abba Täklä Haymanot

Father [Täklä Haymanot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tekle_Haymanot_(d._1755)) was real, as was the Christological debate in 17<sup>th</sup> century Gondor (today's Ethiopia, close to where Hanna lives). In reality, though, he did not keep out of the *Täwaḥədo / Qəbat* debates, but was right in their centre and got in a spot of trouble for it.

Given that there were many other people who shared the same name (three emperors, [1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tekle_Haymanot_of_Gondar), [2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tekle_Haymanot_I), [3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tekle_Haymanot_II) and [a least one saint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tekle_Haymanot)), parhaps our Abba might be another monk who just happened to have that name too. And sense enough to keep his opinions to himself.

### Waman Qispi

Sometime in the late 15th century, [Tawantinsuyu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_Empire), Its supreme ruler, Sapa Inka commanded an empire that stretched  from today's Cali, Colombia, to Santiago, Chile, famously, without needing to use wheels for transport. Macchu Picchu was a private royal estate where Sapa Inka presumably spent time with his retinue when he wasn't in Cusco.

Close to it, in [Chachabamba](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chachabamba), our [khipukamayuq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quipu) made sure that taxes were paid, storehouses properly managed and that matters of the empire ran smoothly, whether the emperor was there or, preferably, whether he wasn't.

### K'in Lakam Chahk

[*Theobroma cacao*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theobroma_cacao) literaly means the food of gods, so K'in's chagrin is easy to understand. Cacao beans were not just used to make cocoa, among the [classical Maya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Maya_civilization) they were also used as currency. 

[Seven temples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plaza_of_the_Seven_Temples) is an area of [Tikal](https://www.penn.museum/sites/expedition/tikal-2/) where K'in's [favourite team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maya_ballgame) is from. And no, historians no longer believe that the losing team would be sacrificed after a game. That would make for pretty uninspired players.

### Shams ad-Din ibn Ashraf Al-Samarqandi

[Shams](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Al-Samarqandi) was a mathematician, astronomer, logician and theologian, who lived in Samarqand approximately from 1250 to 1310. He is known to have written on Euclidean geometry. Zhang Mingyuan is fictional, but a Chinese student from Khanbaliq in today's Beijing would not be far-fetched. It would also make sense for Zhang to have a very different grounding in mathematics and astronomy from what Shams expected.

### Matsuda Reiko

Female priests are not common in Shinto even today, but neither are they unheard of. [Sengū](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seng%C5%AB) is the transfer of [kami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kami) to a temporary sanctuary while the original shrine one is being repaired. As [gūji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shinto_priest, she is responsible for making sure that everything is done correctly.

### Ur-Nana

If you've read anything about the [Sumerians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumer), you know that [*lugal*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lugal) meant king—the first kings in history. But there are serious speculations that the very earliest lugals were military leaders, only chosen when the need arose. Ur-Nana could be one of those.

### Ibrahim ag Moussa

Timbuktu is known as the city of libraries (also the city of 300 saints, but that's a different story). Some of them do go back to the 15th century when Ibrahim practiced law and debated whether to lend his copy of *Jamʿ al-jawāmiʿ*, an important work on legal theory by Tāj al-Dīn al-Subkī, to his impetuous student.

### Marcus Terentius Varro

In 37 BCE, [Varro](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/Varro%2C_Marcus_Terentius) started to write De re rustica, possibly his final work and the only one that is preserved more or less intact. Which is a pity, because he really was called the third great light of classical Rome, along with Cicero and Vergil. All the events of his life mentioned are real, from his tribunate, to the fact that he was a general and an an admiral to Pompeius Magnus, a librarian to Caesar and that he survived Sulla, Pompeius Magnus, Caesar, Brutus, Antony and passed away peacefully in the year that Octavian became Augustus, Rome's first emperor.
